US pigs are contaminated because of rodenticides — and they turned blue. 
In California, wild pigs with “neon blue” fat have triggered alarm among hunters and wildlife officials, revealing a deeper environmental threat. 
These pigs, found in Monterey County, were contaminated by rodenticides—specifically diphacinone—commonly dyed blue for identification. Although this chemical breaks down faster than second-generation poisons, it still lingers in animal tissues long enough to pose risks to predators and humans. 
The striking blue coloration appears in the pigs' fat, while the meat remains normal in color, raising food safety concerns for hunters unaware of the contamination.
The incident underscores the broader ecological consequences of pesticide use. 
Diphacinone, while effective in controlling pests, can inadvertently poison non-target species like mountain lions, foxes, and even endangered animals such as the northern spotted owl. 
California has since tightened regulations on these rodenticides, advocating for safer pest control methods, including integrated pest management. This approach combines physical deterrents, natural predators, and non-toxic repellents to reduce reliance on harmful chemicals. Officials urge anyone encountering blue animals to report the sightings, highlighting the importance of community awareness in preventing further environmental damage.
Source: Koumoundouros, T. (2025, November 30). Wild Pigs Turned 'Neon Blue' in California, Triggering Warnings. ScienceAlert.
